# CC-051 — AU Digital Assets Framework — classify the platform and track thresholds

## Why this is new
This landed during the hiatus and nobody has assessed it. The **Corporations Amendment
(Digital Assets Framework) Bill 2025** passed both houses on 1 April 2026 and received Royal
Assent on 8 April 2026, extending the AFSL regime to digital assets. Coinbase Australia was
granted an AFSL with retail derivatives authorisation days later — the first exchange to get
one directly from ASIC.

## Why it matters here
The Act creates a **Digital Asset Platform (DAP)** category: a facility where an operator
holds digital tokens, for themselves *or on behalf of someone else*. Carbon Contractors
holds agents' USDC in escrow and controls the key that releases it. That is at least
arguably a DAP, and it is a question that needs answering before real money moves — not after.

## The good news
- Small-scale platforms are excluded: **annual transaction volume under $10m AND under
  $5,000 held per client.** At MVP volumes you are far inside both, so launch is not blocked.
- The framework **commences 9 April 2027**, with a 6-month transition after that.

So the practical position is: comfortably exempt today, with roughly 21 months of runway.

## Do
1. Get a considered view on whether the escrow design is a DAP. The non-custodial framing
   (funds in a smart contract, not a platform wallet) is the strongest argument against, but
   the platform controlling the release key cuts the other way. Worth a lawyer, not a guess —
   this note is a flag from a code review, not legal advice.
2. Instrument the two threshold numbers now so you can answer them on demand: annual
   transaction volume, and maximum USDC held per client. Feed into CC-040.
3. Diarise a review well before April 2027.
4. Reflect the position in `/terms` (CC-008).

Related: CC-008, CC-029, CC-040.

## Update 2026-07-25 — Aaron's counter-argument, and where it needs sharpening

**Aaron's position:** this is not custody. The GCP HSM key is non-exportable, so the operator
cannot access, extract or use the funds — that was the entire point of NOR-196. If anyone is
"holding" anything it is Google.

**The half that is right, and is a strong card:** the architecture is genuinely the best available
defence, and it is unusual to be able to demonstrate it with an HSM attestation bundle. Keep it.

**The half that will not survive contact:** the test is not "can the operator extract the key."
It is whether the operator **controls the facility that holds the tokens**. On that framing:

- The USDC sits in `CarbonEscrow`, a contract the operator deployed and owns.
- `completeTaskOnChain`, `resolveDisputeOnChain` and `expireTaskOnChain` are the operator's code,
  invoked through the operator's infrastructure, under the operator's service account. Being
  unable to *copy* the key while being able to *direct it to sign* is still control over disposition.
- `resolveDispute()` is `onlyOwner`. In a dispute the operator decides which party receives the
  money. That is discretionary authority over someone else's funds, and it is much closer to the
  regulatory concern than key storage is.
- "Google is holding them" does not hold — Google holds a signing key and acts on instruction,
  with no view of or interest in the USDC. Comparable to arguing a bank's HSM vendor holds the
  deposits.

### The version of the argument that actually works
Not "I can't reach the key" but **"I can't reach the money, because the contract will not let
me."** That claim is about constraints in the code, not key storage: funds can only ever go to
the worker address recorded on-chain at funding time, or back to the funding agent. No other
destination is reachable by anyone, operator included.

That is a much better argument — and it is exactly what **CC-037** has to establish. If
`resolveDispute` turns out to let the owner direct funds to an arbitrary address, or if any
payout path reads a destination from Supabase, the argument collapses. So CC-037 is not only a
security task; it is the evidence base for this position. Do it before paying for legal advice,
so the lawyer is reasoning about verified behaviour.

### On the thresholds — one non-obvious risk
Aaron's read: $5,000 per client is misaligned with a microtask marketplace, and if jobs get that
big it is a good problem to have. Fair for a single job.

But the exemption needs **both** limbs: annual volume under $10m **and** under $5,000 held per
client. The second is about *aggregate held at a point in time*, not per job. The agentic use
case is precisely one agent batching many small tasks — 30 concurrent tasks at $200 is $6,000
held for that client, and the ceiling binds without a single job exceeding $200.

So the per-client limb can bite far earlier than intuition suggests, and it is driven by
concurrency, not job size. Which makes the instrumentation in item 2 above the actionable part:
track **peak concurrent USDC escrowed per funding agent**, not just totals. Cheap to add now,
awkward to reconstruct later.

Related: CC-037, CC-040.

## Update 2026-08-13 — substantial regulatory research recorded in `ADR-0002` D8

`ADR-0002` D8 carries research done specifically so it is not re-derived later. **Still not legal
advice, and this issue still needs a lawyer** — but read D8 before paying for one, so the questions
asked are the sharp ones.

Headlines, all detailed in the ADR:

- **Turnover, not profit.** The AU small-business threshold is $3M gross income for the whole legal
  entity, prior financial year. Breaking even does not help. Escrowed USDC never passes through the
  platform and is arguably not its income at all — only a platform fee would be. A second benefit of
  the non-custodial design, and an accountant question.
- **No charitable carve-out**, and restructuring as an NFP would constrain the platform fee and the
  `ADR-0001` D8 adjudication market. **Do not structure for the exemption.**
- **The statutory tort ignores the exemption.** Serious invasions of privacy have been actionable
  since 2025 regardless of Privacy Act coverage. Exempt status is no defence if third-party imagery
  leaks. **Highest-weighted risk here**, and exactly what `ADR-0002` D3/D4 mitigate.
- **GDPR is the binding constraint** — no threshold, no carve-out, extraterritorial. One EU worker
  or agent and it applies in full. **Design to GDPR and the Australian question largely resolves
  itself.**
- **Monetisation is the trigger to watch.** The "trades in personal information for a benefit"
  exception applies regardless of turnover, and introducing the platform fee strengthens that limb
  materially. Raise before monetising, not after.
- **Voluntary opt-in (s6EA) is a live option** — converts "too small to be regulated" into "chose to
  be", which is consistent with the whole verifiable-trust pitch.

**On the escrow question this issue already tracks:** `CC-037` verified the load-bearing claim — the
contract permits no destination other than `task.worker` or a refund to `task.agent`, both fixed
on-chain at funding. `ADR-0001` D9 adds the honest counterweight: **the platform is the oracle in
v1.** That authority is bounded and falsifiable (published rules, published inputs, re-runnable
result) rather than eliminated, and v2 makes verdicts permissionless against a bond. Brief the
lawyer on both halves, not just the contract half.

The threshold instrumentation this issue asks for — peak concurrent USDC escrowed per funding agent
— is now specified as a monitor in `CC-085`.

Priority stays **P2**; ~21 months of runway is unchanged.

Related: `ADR-0002` D8, `ADR-0001` D9, `CC-037`, `CC-085`, `CC-081`.

## Update 2026-08-18 — this ticket's "21 months of runway" only covers one of two regimes

Scanning the backlog against `docs/funds_control_aml_gating.md` surfaced that this issue analyses
the **ASIC/Corporations Act "Digital Asset Platform" question only** (Bill 2025, commences
2027-04-09). It does not cover the **AUSTRAC AML/CTF Tranche 2 reform** (virtual asset service
provider registration), which is a different regulator, a different Act, and a materially closer
clock — full obligations for that regime commenced **2026-07-01**, already passed as of this
writing.

Filed separately as `CC-098` rather than folded in here, specifically so "21 months of runway"
above continues to mean what it says for *this* regime and does not get read as covering both.
Do not treat this ticket's conclusion as clearing the AUSTRAC question — it doesn't address it.

Related addition: `CC-098`, `CC-099` (sanctions screening, independently justified regardless of
how either classification question resolves).
